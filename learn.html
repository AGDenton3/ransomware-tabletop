NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 (April 2025)

Incident response as part of managing risk

NIST Special Publication 800-61 is the U.S. government's main guidance on incident response. Revision 3, published in April 2025, is written as a “Community Profile” of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0. Instead of treating incident response as a separate checklist that starts when an alarm goes off, it describes incident response as part of an organization's everyday cybersecurity risk management, organized around the six CSF 2.0 Functions. [NIST SP 800-61r3; NIST CSF 2.0]

What changed

From a four-phase cycle to six Functions

Revision 2 (2012) described a four-phase incident response lifecycle. Many courses and playbooks still use it, so the game shows each decision's Rev. 2 phase as a secondary label. [NIST SP 800-61r2]

Rev. 2 (2012): lifecycle

  1. Preparation
  2. Detection & Analysis
  3. Containment, Eradication & Recovery
  4. Post-Incident Activity, feeding back into Preparation

Rev. 3 (2025): CSF 2.0 Functions

  • Before an incident: Govern, Identify and Protect cover much of what Rev. 2 called Preparation.
  • During an incident: Detect, Respond and Recover.
  • Lessons learned: improvement runs through all the Functions continuously rather than coming only at the end.

Why the change matters

Rev. 3 makes clear that how an incident goes is mostly decided before it starts: by governance, inventory and safeguards. That is the point of the game's “Preparation would have changed this” debrief section. [NIST SP 800-61r3]

The hardest call

Should you pay?

The game doesn't give a verdict, and neither does this page. These are the considerations, each from a primary source. Decisions like this belong with leadership, counsel and law enforcement, not with the IT lead alone.

No guarantee of recovery

The FBI says it does not support paying a ransom, partly because paying does not guarantee an organization will get its data back. [FBI IC3] In the game, paid decryptors are slow and partial.

No guarantee of deletion

With double extortion, attackers steal data before encrypting it. A “deletion log” from a criminal cannot be verified, and the notification duties for stolen data remain whether or not you pay. [HHS OCR; Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 521.053]

Sanctions risk

Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) warns that paying a sanctioned person or group can violate U.S. sanctions, and that penalties can be imposed on a strict-liability basis, even if you didn't know. The advisory states that the U.S. government strongly discourages paying. [OFAC 2021]

Funding the next attack

The FBI notes that payment may embolden attackers to target more organizations and fund further criminal activity. [FBI IC3]

Law enforcement wants to hear from you either way

The FBI asks victims to report incidents whether or not they pay. OFAC treats prompt reporting to and cooperation with law enforcement as mitigating factors. [FBI PSA 2019; OFAC 2021]

Why some organizations still consider it

The FBI acknowledges that executives facing an inability to operate will evaluate all their options. For a hospital or utility, how long an outage lasts can be a safety issue, which is why tested backups and manual workarounds, decided in advance, matter so much. [FBI PSA 2019; #StopRansomware Guide]
